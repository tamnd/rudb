# The road to ten times

Notes written on 14 September 2026, after auditing tamnd/rudb-bench#71 and re-running the ClickBench ladder from 1k to 1m rows on `gamingpc-wsl` against rudb at commit `cfdf975`, which is 0.3.6 plus the three top N changes.

The goal has not changed. rudb is meant to be ten times faster than DuckDB and to use ten times less resource on any benchmark. What these notes do is say what that means as a number, where the number is today, and which of the F milestones actually moves it.

## The four files

1. [`01-audit.md`](01-audit.md) is the audit of the root cause report in rudb-bench#71. Five of its claims hold up against the source, one of its conclusions is size dependent and stated too broadly, and it leaves out the largest line item on the board.
2. [`02-budget.md`](02-budget.md) is the arithmetic. What ten times costs in CPU seconds and in bytes, split per operator and per query, with the reduction each one has to deliver.
3. [`03-roadmap.md`](03-roadmap.md) is the F series re-ordered by measured cost, with the critical path and the first ten pull requests on it.
4. [`04-loop.md`](04-loop.md) is the development loop. Run 1k to 1m on every change because it takes four minutes, and hold anything that claims a number to 10m.
5. [`05-duckdb-baseline.md`](05-duckdb-baseline.md) is what DuckDB costs on the same box, which is the number every target here is a fraction of.
6. [`06-partitioned-aggregation.md`](06-partitioned-aggregation.md) is DuckDB's two phase radix partitioned aggregate hash table, read out of their source and their write up, and what it means for F5.
7. [`07-the-root-causes.md`](07-the-root-causes.md) is the five causes the profile actually names, as opposed to the ones it was assumed to name.
8. [`08-where-the-scan-goes.md`](08-where-the-scan-goes.md) splits the scan's own seconds, which were 57.7 percent of everything and unattributed.
9. [`09-the-measured-work-list.md`](09-the-measured-work-list.md) is the work sorted by measured cost rather than by plan order.
10. [`10-what-the-encoder-costs.md`](10-what-the-encoder-costs.md) is `cargo xtask encode`, the per candidate split of the encoder's seconds, and its thread scaling.
11. [`11-how-duckdb-reads-parquet.md`](11-how-duckdb-reads-parquet.md) is `cargo xtask parquet`, both engines over the same file, the two root causes it separates, and the six item work list that follows.
12. [`12-the-chunk-and-the-page.md`](12-the-chunk-and-the-page.md) is what a chunk costs before it holds any data, why the in memory table cannot hand one out without copying it, and the four changes that follow from those two numbers.
13. [`13-what-tpch-costs-in-instructions.md`](13-what-tpch-costs-in-instructions.md) is the first TPC-H note and the first one measured in instructions retired rather than seconds. It says rudb does 2.2 times the work DuckDB does on SF1, that most of the engine is already past ten times, that all of the gap is in one operator, and that closing it entirely still leaves the goal out of reach, so it separates the work that reaches parity from the one idea that reaches ten times.
14. [`14-what-a-group-costs.md`](14-what-a-group-costs.md) takes the top of note 13's work list apart. It measures what one more aggregate call costs at six groups and at a million and a half, finds that the added cost is mostly per group rather than per row, and splits an added call by instructions into starting a state, finishing one into a value, reading the column and the addition itself. The largest single item is the finalise, which builds a `Value` per group per call and then copies it into the output vector, and that is the one change it moves to the front of note 13's order.

15. [`15-what-a-row-costs.md`](15-what-a-row-costs.md) is the other half of note 14, measured per row rather than per group, and it is the half TPC-H pays for. It shows that scan, filter and a small group table are only 1.33 times behind, that every added aggregate call costs 60 to 75 instructions a row against duckdb's 3 to 35, and that neither the column's cardinality nor its packing changes that. It counts the scatter's loop out of the disassembly at forty five instructions a row, of which seven are the query, and it finds a grouped `min` over a `DATE` costing 776 instructions a row because the type was missing from one list.

16. [`16-a-key-that-is-not-an-integer.md`](16-a-key-that-is-not-an-integer.md) is note 15's defect one operator over. A group key whose type was not one of five went to a run of tagged values, and the compare that runs once per input row per probe step built a value on each side of it, which over a packed column allocates. A date key was nine times behind duckdb and a string key was one and a bit. Then it finds the reason every fixed width key was left at three and a half times, which is that the direct map read the shape a filter leaves as a dictionary of rows rather than as the packed page underneath, so it built one map entry per row of the page and rebuilt it every chunk. It also writes down the two ideas that were measured and dropped on the way there, and the hash bug the new test found.

17. [`17-what-a-join-filter-costs.md`](17-what-a-join-filter-costs.md) is the symbol at the top of the profile once note 16 is done, which is the runtime Bloom filter a hash join hands down to the scan under its other side. It counts the probe's loop body out of the disassembly at 62 instructions a row, takes six of them off, and writes down what the other 56 are made of and which of them a format change could take. It then does the same for `table::hash`, which is the largest single symbol on the suite, and finds that its flat column fast paths are looking for a shape a filtered query never produces, because a filter hands on a dictionary of the rows that got through.
18. [`18-the-rows-a-filter-kept.md`](18-the-rows-a-filter-kept.md) is two findings that are not faster loops but work done for a shape that was never coming. The aggregate took room in advance in tables that only pass groups on, which made q18 a quarter slower and seven times larger in memory, and the hash kernel's fast paths never ran behind a filter because a filter hands on a dictionary of the rows it kept. It gives the numbers for both fixes and lists the kernels where the second one is likely to repeat.

[`../storage-v2/`](../storage-v2/) is the format these notes keep arriving at, designed from the queries rather than from the file, with the size target worked out against the real `hits.parquet` instead of assumed.

## The short version

At one million rows rudb answers the 41 ClickBench queries it can answer in 4.568 seconds of query time against DuckDB's 0.533, which is 9.25 times behind, and it does it in 172 MiB against DuckDB's 309, which is 1.8 times ahead. Ten times means 53 milliseconds and 31 MiB.

We are not losing because of any one slow loop. We are losing because of four things that multiply together, and no single one of them closes the gap:

| what | what it costs today | what it has to become |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One thread where DuckDB uses six | 1.0 core against 6.5 | 16 effective cores or better |
| Parquet decoded inside every query | 52.9 percent of our CPU | a native format read once at load |
| A group key that is a `Vec<Value>` on the heap | 27.6 percent of our CPU and all of our peak memory | a code or a packed integer in an arena |
| A per query floor of 3.2 ms at one million rows | below DuckDB's 12 ms, above the 1.3 ms target | metadata read lazily, no fixed work per column |

The first is F4, the second is F2, the third is F5, and the fourth is F2 again. F1 owns the kernels underneath all of them and is worth about 10 percent on its own.

The order that follows from the measurement is F5 before F4 before F2, because a parallel driver over an aggregate that refuses a second instance buys nothing, and because the compact group key is both the thing F4 needs to merge and the thing the memory target needs. That is a change from the order the milestones were written in, and it is the whole point of these notes.

## The short version on TPC-H, added 22 September 2026

Note 13 is the same exercise on the other benchmark and it lands somewhere different. Measured in instructions retired rather than seconds, rudb does 2.2 times the work DuckDB does on TPC-H SF1. Most of the engine is already past ten times: the process floor is 19 times cheaper, an ungrouped sum beats DuckDB, and `count`, `min` and `max` are answered out of the committed directory without reading the page at all. Every bit of the 2.2 times is in the grouped aggregate, and it is there twice over, once per aggregate call because the state is a tagged enum in a side vector rather than a row in the table, and once per row because the partition copies the input instead of the table.

The number that decides what to do is this. Ten times DuckDB on the suite means 2.39 G of instructions against the 52.98 G rudb spends today, so the engine has to do twenty two times less work. Closing the whole aggregate gap is worth 2.2 of that. The rest has to come from not touching the rows, which means deciding the group at load time rather than at query time, and that is the same thesis [`../engine-v2/13-encoded-execution.md`](../engine-v2/13-encoded-execution.md) argues from the layout side.
19. [`19-a-group-that-cannot-grow.md`](19-a-group-that-cannot-grow.md) is the aggregate over a key the table is stored in order of. A group whose key the rows have moved past is finished, so every run strictly inside a sorted chunk is closed without a hash, a probe or a merge, and only the two ends of each chunk go through the table. It explains where the promise of order comes from and why a chunk that breaks it costs time and never a wrong answer. q18 went from 6.0 G to 4.1 G instructions, and its inner aggregate now does less work than DuckDB's.
20. [`20-a-total-per-chunk.md`](20-a-total-per-chunk.md) is the grouped sum, mean and count over a handful of groups. A chunk is added up in a local per group and each group's total reaches its accumulator once per chunk, so a row no longer pays for the state lookup, the tag and a checked 128 bit add. It explains why the local add needs no check and when the path pays. q01 went from 4.27 G to 3.76 G instructions.
21. [`21-codes-in-bulk.md`](21-codes-in-bulk.md) is reading a packed column. A third of q01 was reading packed codes one at a time, each one a bounds check and a branch on whether it straddled two words. Codes are now unpacked sixty four at a time by code the width is a constant in, and the sums, the casts and the filters loop over plain numbers. It also covers the fold's key lookup, now one pass, and two checks that a dictionary's constructor had already made. q01 went from 3.76 G to 3.47 G instructions and the suite from 44.6 G to 43.9 G, but the cycles fell only 4 percent, and the note says why. Against the main it merged into, q01 went from 3.98 G to 3.50 G and its cycles fell 13 percent.
22. [`22-pages-between-queries.md`](22-pages-between-queries.md) is the page pool. Every reader of a database keeps its pages under one budget in bytes, half the memory limit, and lets them go by the clock, with the old four stripes a column kept as a floor. It measures what storage was worth before building it: keeping every page took two thirds off the system time of the TPC-H suite and nothing off the user time, and dropping checksums saved nothing. The gap to DuckDB is in joins and grouped aggregates, which is where the next notes go.
23. [`23-filters-through-joins.md`](23-filters-through-joins.md) is runtime filters that pass through the inner and semi joins between the join that built them and the scan they belong to. Before, a filter from a join that drove from another join had nowhere to go. It also covers the exact bitmap that takes a filter's place when the build keys are integers close together, which costs a subtraction a row instead of a hash. q05 went from 2.3 times DuckDB's CPU to 1.5, q18 from 1.2 to 0.8, and q02 is now under DuckDB.
24. [`24-adds-that-wait.md`](24-adds-that-wait.md) is the grouped sum's row loop measured in cycles. Over four groups a row's add read the total the row before it had just stored, so every row waited on the one before. Each group now keeps four locals picked by the row's position, and a chunk counts its rows per group once for every call rather than once per call. Ten q01 runs on one thread went from 15.0 G to 13.5 G cycles, with 3 percent more instructions.
25. [`25-arithmetic-in-one-loop.md`](25-arithmetic-in-one-loop.md) is decimal arithmetic run as one loop. A chain of adds, subtracts and multiplies over decimal columns is compiled into a small program over 256 row buffers, proven free of overflow once per chunk from the ranges the columns already know, and run with no check per row, falling back to the ordinary steps when the proof fails. A filter now also compacts a packed column by unpacking only the kept rows in bulk. Ten q01 runs on one thread went from 35.4 G to 32.2 G instructions, q14 from 28 ms to 16 ms, and the suite on gpc from 593 ms to 583 ms against DuckDB's 1289 ms.
26. [`26-cheap-filters-first.md`](26-cheap-filters-first.md) is the order of a scan's filters. The native scan ran the filter pushed into it and narrowed every column before it ran the joins' runtime filters, so q03 unpacked about fifty rows for each one a join read, which got worse once #1576 made narrowing unpack packed columns right away. Exact bitmaps now run first, and Bloom filters run first once the pushed filter has been measured keeping more than half the rows. Seven affected queries went from 10.60 G to 8.25 G instructions, and q03, q04, q07 and q21 are now under DuckDB.
27. [`27-tables-the-driving-side-narrows.md`](27-tables-the-driving-side-narrows.md) is a join leaving out of its hash table the gathered rows no driving row can match, known from the exact bitmap of an inner or semi join below it on the same column, which a join settled by exact rows now makes when one above asks. q09 went from 2.80 G to 2.27 G instructions and from 289 ms to 226 ms of CPU, under DuckDB's 254 ms.
28. [`28-bitmaps-that-fit-the-cache.md`](28-bitmaps-that-fit-the-cache.md) is the exact bitmap of a join's build keys taken whenever it fits in 32 KiB, however few keys it holds, instead of only up to 64 bits a key. A bit test in the first level cache is cheaper than the Bloom filter's hash, and the bitmap lets through only rows that match. q17 went from 1.245 G to 0.817 G instructions, q08 from 0.895 G to 0.674 G and q19 from 0.858 G to 0.588 G, and the suite is now 24.54 G against DuckDB's 25.41 G.
29. [`29-keys-that-index.md`](29-keys-that-index.md) is a join on one integer key whose keys sit close together keeping one head a key in place of a hash table, the key minus the smallest being the place. A probe subtracts and checks a range instead of hashing and comparing. q13 went from 2.88 G to 2.60 G instructions and from 359 ms to 298 ms of CPU, under DuckDB's 327 ms, and the suite from 24.50 G to 23.93 G.
30. [`30-strings-after-the-filters.md`](30-strings-after-the-filters.md) is a scan reading the string columns nothing filters on only after its filters have run, and only at the rows they kept. A compressed text page steps over the values that are not wanted by their lengths and never decompresses them. q10 went from 1.74 G to 1.45 G instructions and from 173 ms to 156 ms of CPU, and the suite from 23.87 G to 23.57 G against DuckDB's 25.38 G.
31. [`31-sums-below-the-join.md`](31-sums-below-the-join.md) is an optimizer pass summing below an inner join when the join only brings in strings to group by, so the wide grouping sees one row per group of the narrow key instead of one per joined row. It only fires when the other input is an unfiltered scan, after the first version made q05 read every row a selective join was about to drop. q10 went from 1.449 G to 1.263 G instructions, under DuckDB's 1.454 G, and the suite from 23.56 G to 23.36 G.
32. [`32-columns-the-filter-read-last.md`](32-columns-the-filter-read-last.md) is a scan leaving out of its narrowing the packed columns only its own filter reads, found by walking up the plan to the aggregate or projection that hides the scan's columns. q01 was unpacking six million ship dates a query to throw them away. On one thread q01 went from 2.59 G to 2.41 G instructions and q09 from 2.48 G to 2.31 G.
33. [`33-filters-that-mark.md`](33-filters-that-mark.md) is a filter directly under a grouped aggregate that keeps at least three quarters of a chunk marking the rows it kept instead of cutting every column to them. The aggregate cuts only its keys, spreads the slots back out with the dropped rows pointing at no group, and reads the arguments over the whole chunk with no gather. q01 went from 27.65 G to 25.84 G instructions over ten runs on one thread, and from 2.869 G to 2.686 G in the suite.
34. [`34-an-average-off-a-sum.md`](34-an-average-off-a-sum.md) is an optimizer pass answering `avg(x)` from a `sum(x)` the same aggregate already has and a `count(x)`, divided in a projection above through the same function the mean state finishes with, so the bits are the same. q01 was adding its quantity and price columns up twice. On one thread, on top of #1700, q01 went from 2.05 G to 1.87 G instructions, under DuckDB's 2.14 G.
35. [`35-groups-answered-from-their-runs.md`](35-groups-answered-from-their-runs.md) is a grouping over a key the table is stored in order of answering the groups a chunk closes straight from their runs, the key gathered at the first row of each run and each count or total added up where it lies, instead of putting every group through a table and an accumulator per call. `GROUP BY l_orderkey` over lineitem went from 1.72 G to 0.86 G instructions, under DuckDB's 1.14 G, q18 from 2.85 G to 2.03 G, and the suite from 28.23 G to 27.46 G.
36. [`36-strings-the-filter-read-last.md`](36-strings-the-filter-read-last.md) is a scan handing up a string column only its own filter reads as a null constant, which a gather now keeps as a constant, and a native varchar page checked for UTF-8 in one pass over its strings instead of once per string. The order comment in q13 was being copied out by the join above and checked a string at a time. On one thread q13 went from 2.34 G to 2.11 G instructions against DuckDB's 1.59 G.
37. [`37-fsst-a-store-a-symbol.md`](37-fsst-a-store-a-symbol.md) is a native FSST chunk decoded with one eight byte store a symbol into room made ahead of it, instead of growing a vector a symbol at a time and cutting it back. The order comment in q13 cost about six instructions a byte to decompress. On one thread q13 went from 2.11 G to 1.86 G instructions against DuckDB's 1.58 G, and at default threads it is now under DuckDB.
38. [`38-strings-sorted-by-rank.md`](38-strings-sorted-by-rank.md) is a sort on string keys writing each string into the normalized key as its rank among the strings of that key, computed with one sort per key once every row is in, instead of falling back to a `Vec<Value>` per row. q16's final sort of 18,314 rows was a fifth of the query. On one thread q16 went from 0.52 G to 0.435 G instructions, and at default threads it is under DuckDB.
39. [`39-codes-through-the-join.md`](39-codes-through-the-join.md) is the gathered side of a join laying out a column whose pieces are all codes into one table wide dictionary as codes over that dictionary, instead of flattening them into strings. The grouping and the sort above the join then read codes. On one thread q16 went from 0.432 G to 0.328 G instructions, level with DuckDB, and q09 from 2.015 G to 1.855 G.
40. [`40-packed-dates-compared-in-one-pass.md`](40-packed-dates-compared-in-one-pass.md) is a comparison of two bit packed columns unpacking both in blocks and writing the selection in the same pass, instead of reading a code at a time into a flag per row and picking the rows in a second pass, and a later conjunct over a flat column and a literal getting the one pass loop the first conjunct had. On one thread q12 went from 1.084 G to 0.856 G instructions against DuckDB's 0.916 G, and q06 from 0.491 G to 0.430 G.
