# Research landscape, September 2026

Everything in this document was checked against a primary source in September 2026. Where a number is quoted it is either from the paper's own abstract or evaluation, or recomputed locally from public benchmark artifacts, and document 03 says which. Where I could not verify something I say so rather than repeating it.

The organizing question is not "what is interesting" but "what does this force in the design." Each subsection ends with the forcing consequence and the document that acts on it.

## 1.1 The result the project is built on

[**Bespoke OLAP: Synthesizing Workload-Specific One-size-fits-one Database Engines**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02001). Wehrstein, Eckmann, Jasny and Binnig, TU Darmstadt and DFKI, PVLDB 19(11), VLDB 2026. Code at [DataManagementLab/BespokeOLAP](https://github.com/DataManagementLab/BespokeOLAP).

The pipeline generates a C++ query engine specialized to a fixed workload, iterating on compile, deploy, validate and profile until it stops improving. Roughly 11,600 lines of generated C++ per engine, 10 to 30 hours wall clock, 276 to 303 dollars of API cost. Baseline is DuckDB 1.4.1 on an AMD EPYC 9654P with 768 GB, single threaded and core pinned, data preloaded from Parquet.

Headline: 11.17x over DuckDB on TPC-H, 45.33x on CEB, 7.24x and 9.56x over Umbra. At 64 threads the numbers fall to 7.65x and 23.97x over DuckDB, which is itself informative about how much of the win is parallel-friendly.

The ablation is the reason this document exists. Constrained to flat struct-of-arrays storage and allowed to optimize only code: **1.26x on TPC-H, 0.57x on CEB**. Allowed to specialize storage: **12.35x and 51.40x**.

The paper catalogs the specializations by frequency across the query set, and the distribution is worth reading closely because it is a shopping list. Storage side: dictionary encoding on 25% of columns, physical sort orders on 18.75%, precomputed derived columns used by 36.84% of queries, 16-bit date offsets on 16.94%, flat string arenas with offset arrays on 10.94%. Structural side: bitmap semi-joins in 73.68% of queries, hash indices giving O(1) join lookups in 47.37%, index nested-loop joins in 42.11%, inline fused aggregation avoiding materialization in 97.37%, dense-key aggregation in 55.26%, zone map pruning over 64K-row blocks. Low level side: `restrict` aliasing hints in 50%, integer-scaled arithmetic replacing floating point in 44.74%, manual unrolling in 13.16%, branchless range checks in 18.42%, software prefetch in 10.53%.

Notice which bucket is which. The low-level list is the entire content of most "we made our engine fast" blog posts and it is the bucket the ablation prices at 26 percent. The storage and structure lists are the order of magnitude.

**Forces:** the thesis in document 02, and the fact that documents 05 and 06 are the two longest in the series. Also forces a specific research program: for each item in their catalog, decide whether a general engine can select it at runtime from samples and statistics, or whether it fundamentally needs the workload known in advance. Document 09.3 is that table.

## 1.2 The storage and compression line

[**The FastLanes File Format**](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p4629-afroozeh.pdf). Afroozeh and Boncz, CWI, PVLDB 18(11) pages 4629 to 4643, VLDB 2025. Code at [cwida/FastLanes](https://github.com/cwida/fastlanes), artifacts at [cwida/fastlanes-vldb2025](https://github.com/cwida/fastlanes-vldb2025). Now also Afroozeh's 2026 VU Amsterdam thesis.

The argument is that Parquet and ORC were designed in 2013 and that both hardware and workloads have moved. FastLanes uses only lightweight encodings that are fully data parallel, meaning they decode with SIMD or on a GPU without branches, and it refuses general-purpose block compressors like Snappy and Zstd entirely. It cascades encodings through a flexible expression mechanism, and the same mechanism gives **multi-column compression**, which exploits correlation between columns. Columnar formats have historically been unable to do that at all, and it is the single most interesting idea in the paper for our purposes. The unit is a 1024-value vector, decompression is vector-at-a-time so the working set stays tiny, and compressed vectors are directly addressable rather than requiring materialization.

The lineage matters because each piece is separately proven. [The FastLanes Compression Layout](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.14778/3598581.3598587) (PVLDB 16(9), 2023) is the interleaved bit-packing layout that decodes over 100 billion integers per second with scalar code that auto-vectorizes. [ALP](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3626717) (Afroozeh, Kuffó and Boncz, SIGMOD 2024) is adaptive lossless floating point, which encodes doubles that originated as decimals into integers by multiplying by a power of ten and then frame-of-reference packing them, with a separate front-bits path (ALP-RD) for genuine reals. [FSST](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol13/p2649-boncz.pdf) (Boncz, Neumann and Leis, VLDB 2020) is the static symbol table string compressor that gives random access to individual strings without decompressing a block. [Accelerating GPU Data Processing Using FastLanes Compression](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3662010.3663450) (DaMoN 2024) is the GPU decode path.

Independent confirmation that this line is winning: DuckDB v2.0 makes `DICT_FSST` a default encoding, and the Apache Parquet community is standardizing both ALP and FSST as first-class encodings in 2026, specifically because high-cardinality strings and float measurements are the two column types that dominate byte size and compress worst under the 2013 encoding set.

**Forces:** the entire encoding set in document 06 and the 1024-value vector size decision in document 07. Multi-column compression is the mechanism the resource axis in document 02 depends on, and it is open question one in document 19 because nobody has published what it does to ClickBench hits specifically.

[**Vortex**](https://vortex.dev/), formerly Spiral, now an incubation-stage project at LF AI and Data. Rust, extensible encoding registry, FSST and ALP and FastLanes bit-packing, compute kernels that operate on encoded data, AVX2 gather for `take`, file format stable from 0.36.0 onward.

Vortex is the closest thing to a Rust implementation of the ideas in document 06 and it is a genuine prior art check on the whole approach. It is also a cautionary data point, and the caution is measured rather than rhetorical. Vortex's own claims are 100x faster random access and 10 to 20x faster scans than Parquet at similar compression ratio. On ClickBench at `c6a.4xlarge`, `duckdb-vortex` totals **40.99 seconds hot against native `duckdb` at 26.25**, and `datafusion-vortex` totals **91.30 against `datafusion` at 45.57**. On a full-scan analytical workload, wrapping a good engine in Vortex currently costs about 1.6x and 2.0x respectively.

That gap between the claim and the benchmark is the most useful single fact in this document. It says that a format optimized for random access and for compressed representation does not automatically win at scan, and that the win has to be engineered at the boundary between the format and the execution engine rather than inside the format. Document 06.7 is about that boundary and document 19 open question three is about whether we should be building on Vortex rather than beside it.

## 1.3 Where the compiled-versus-vectorized argument actually stands

The canonical reference is still Kersten, Leis, Kemper, Neumann, Pavlo and Boncz, [**Everything you always wanted to know about compiled and vectorized queries but were afraid to ask**](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf), PVLDB 11(13), 2018, which built both models inside one system with identical algorithms and data structures. Its conclusion holds up: both are efficient, they have different strengths, and because most OLAP queries are data-access bound rather than compute bound, SIMD does not settle the argument in vectorization's favour the way people assume.

What has changed since is the compile latency story, and that is now the real axis.

[**TPDE: A Fast Adaptable Compiler Back-End Framework**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22610), 2025. One analysis pass then one compilation pass fusing instruction selection, register allocation and encoding. **8 to 24x faster compilation than LLVM -O0 on SPECint 2017, with run-time performance on par with -O0.** x86-64 and AArch64. The authors built a complete LLVM back end on it to prove generality, and note that for domain-specific IRs such as query plans, skipping the translation to LLVM IR reduces latency further.

[**Compile-Time Analysis of Compiler Frameworks for Query Compilation**](https://home.cit.tum.de/~engelke/pubs/2403-cgo.pdf), CGO 2024, measured inside Umbra. Cranelift compiles only 20 to 35 percent faster than LLVM and 16x slower than Umbra's own single-pass back end. It also documents the practical friction: Cranelift's JIT library does not emit unwind information so DWARF CFI has to be produced by hand, and the C++ to Rust interop cost roughly 1.6k lines of the 4k line integration.

Umbra's own stack, from [Umbra: A Disk-Based System with In-Memory Performance](https://db.in.tum.de/~freitag/papers/p29-neumann-cidr20.pdf) (Neumann and Freitag, CIDR 2020) and the follow-on work, is three tiers: an Umbra IR bytecode interpreter, a Flying Start single-pass x86 emitter that performs on par with LLVM -O0, and LLVM for the longest-running queries, with the engine tracking progress at runtime to decide when to promote. [Dynamic Blocks](https://db.in.tum.de/~neumann/) avoids recompilation by emitting all variants into the generated code and switching between them, which the paper measures at more than 2x on data-centric code.

The opposite pole is Photon, from Behm and colleagues, [**Photon: A Fast Query Engine for Lakehouse Systems**](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~matei/papers/2022/sigmod_photon.pdf), SIGMOD 2022. Databricks chose interpreted vectorized specifically because adaptivity is easier when dynamic dispatch is fundamental: Photon builds per-batch metadata and picks kernels that skip null handling when a batch has no nulls, skip position-list indirection when no rows are inactive, and take an ASCII fast path when a string batch is all ASCII. A code-generating engine would have to compile a prohibitive number of branches or recompile mid-query. Velox, [Pedreira et al., PVLDB 15(12), 2022](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol15/p3372-pedreira.pdf), reaches the same place from Meta's side with filter and conjunct reordering, key normalization, dynamic filter pushdown and adaptive column prefetching, and the paper is explicit that codegen support in Velox remains experimental and that the vectorized-versus-codegen adaptivity question is open.

**Forces:** the four-tier model in document 08. Cranelift is the middle tier because in a Rust engine its interop cost is zero, but the CGO number means we should not expect it to win on latency alone, and TPDE is the design we copy if the M6 experiment says the single-pass tier is needed. The Photon and Velox results force document 07 to carry per-batch adaptive kernels regardless of which compilation tier is active, because that adaptivity is orthogonal and it is cheap.

## 1.4 Joins, and why the planner stops being the risk

[**Debunking the Myth of Join Ordering: Toward Robust SQL Analytics**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15181). Zhao, Su, Yang, Yu, Koutris and Zhang, SIGMOD 2025, [ACM DL 10.1145/3725283](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3725283).

Predicate Transfer (Yang et al., 2024) speeds up semi-join reduction by building Bloom filters instead of full hash tables, but it inherits none of Yannakakis's guarantee because its heuristic of directing each edge from smaller table to larger can produce schedules that do not fully reduce. Robust Predicate Transfer fixes this with two algorithms: **LargestRoot** builds a maximum spanning tree on the weighted join graph to guarantee full reduction, and **SafeSubjoin** exploits the result that every Cartesian-free subjoin of a natural join query is safe if and only if the query is gamma-acyclic. Integrated into DuckDB and evaluated on TPC-H, JOB, TPC-DS and DSB: **orders of magnitude better join-order robustness, and 1.5x per-query geometric mean end to end**.

The conclusion the authors draw is the one that matters here. With RPT in place, a future optimizer could restrict its search to left-deep plans, or pick a random join order, and be far more tolerant of cardinality estimation error.

[**I Can't Believe It's Not Yannakakis: Pragmatic Bitmap Filters in Microsoft SQL Server**](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p29-zhao.pdf). Zhao, Tian, Alotaibi, Ding, Bruno, Camacho-Rodríguez, Papadimos, Cervantes Juárez, Galindo-Legaria and Curino, CIDR 2026. Shows that SQL Server's existing bitmap filter machinery already approximates Yannakakis in practice: hash join builds bitmaps alongside hash tables and pushes them into the probe-side subplan, and chaining hash joins cascades them through multi-joins, which under the assumption of exact semijoins corresponds to Yannakakis's bottom-up pass. Measured against a hand-picked optimal semijoin schedule, some queries reject over 99 percent of non-joining rows. The identified gap is structural: SQL Server does a single pass of bitmap pre-filtering and does not circulate bitmaps across the whole join graph the way multi-pass semijoin does.

[**On the Vexing Difficulty of Evaluating IN Predicates**](https://www.cidrdb.org/cidr2026//papers.html). Birler and Neumann, CIDR 2026. A narrow paper about a case that shows up constantly in generated SQL and is handled badly by nearly everyone.

[**Simple, Efficient, and Robust Hash Tables for Join Processing**](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3662010.3663442). Birler, Schmidt, Fent and Neumann, DaMoN 2024. The current reference design in the Umbra lineage: a chained table with tag bits packed into the pointer, which resolves most negative probes without a cache miss. This is the design document 07 adopts.

[**To Partition, or Not to Partition, That is the Join Question in a Real System**](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3448016.3452831). Bandle, Giceva and Neumann, SIGMOD 2021. Still the canonical evaluation of radix-partitioned versus non-partitioned hash join inside a production engine, and still the answer to anyone claiming radix partitioning is unconditionally better.

[**Global Hash Tables Strike Back! An Analysis of Parallel GROUP BY Aggregation**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04153), 2025. Argues that fully concurrent global aggregation is a viable alternative to the partitioned approach every engine currently uses, and derives implementer guidelines. It also names the tension precisely: join tables are optimized around probe-side existence checks, aggregation tables are not, and the concurrent designs that make joins fast are generally incompatible with what concurrent aggregation needs.

**Forces:** RPT is in document 09 from M4. Document 07's hash table is the DaMoN 2024 tagged-pointer design for joins and a separate design for aggregation, and document 19 open question five is whether the global-hash-table result changes that. The IN-predicate paper forces a specific fast path in document 10.

## 1.5 Storage hardware, I/O, and what the machine actually is

[**Cloudspecs: Cloud Hardware Evolution Through the Looking Glass**](https://www.cs.cit.tum.de/fileadmin/w00cfj/dis/papers/cloudspecs-final.pdf). Steinert, Kuschewski and Leis, TUM, CIDR 2026. Tool at [TUM-DIS/cloudspecs](https://github.com/TUM-DIS/cloudspecs), which is itself built on DuckDB-WASM.

Ten years of AWS, 2015 to 2025, measured in performance per dollar. Network bandwidth per dollar improved by an order of magnitude. CPU and DRAM gains were much smaller. **NVMe storage performance per dollar has been flat since 2016**, which the authors flag as the most surprising finding and give a dedicated discussion. Maximum core count grew by an order of magnitude, to 448 on `u7in`. Smaller cloud providers undercut the big three by up to 5x on commodity VMs.

[**io_uring for High-Performance DBMSs: When and How to Use It**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04859). TU Darmstadt, PVLDB 19(1), 2026. Evaluates io_uring in a storage-bound buffer manager and in network-bound shuffle. The stepwise result on a 3.7 GHz CPU with PCIe 5 NVMe: POSIX and libaio at 16.5k tx/s, plus batched writeback 19.0k, plus async execution 183k. Registered buffers avoid per-request page pinning and kernel-user copies and add about 11 percent to 238k tx/s. NVMe passthrough via `OP_URING_CMD` bypasses the generic block layer. SQPOLL plus IOPOLL lets one thread exceed 1M IOPS at queue depth 1. The validation was a 14 percent improvement to PostgreSQL's own io_uring integration. The blunt conclusion is that naive use gives modest gains and only a design that exploits the advanced features more than doubles throughput.

[**LeanStore: A High-Performance Storage Engine for NVMe SSDs**](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p4536-leis.pdf), PVLDB 17. 12.5M IOPS across 8 Samsung PM1733 drives, over 1M TPC-C transactions per second at a 4 TB dataset. Umbra's buffer manager is LeanStore with variable-size pages in exponentially growing size classes starting at 64 KiB, which removes the indirection layer that a fixed-page buffer manager forces on large objects, plus pointer swizzling so that resident pages cost a pointer dereference rather than a hash lookup.

The practitioner counterweight is worth reading before committing to io_uring: Conviva published two posts in September 2026 on replacing mmap with io_uring in a Rust query engine, and part one ends with the compio-based rewrite running **60 percent slower** than the mmap baseline, 16 seconds against 13.6, even after O_DIRECT and working around Arrow's copy-happy buffer construction. Their fio floor was 20.2 GiB/s across 32 NVMe drives against mmap's 3.44 GB/s, so the hardware was there and the software was not. Part two concludes their entire concurrency and submission architecture was wrong. They also flag, correctly, that the PVLDB io_uring guidance is derived from 4 KB page buffer managers and may not transfer to large-block columnar reads.

**Forces:** document 05's I/O layer is designed around large sequential reads with a small number of deep queues, not around 4 KB random access, and the io_uring path is behind a feature flag with mmap and pread as measured alternatives rather than as legacy fallbacks. The Cloudspecs flat-NVMe finding forces the resource axis to be about bytes on disk and bytes moved, not about IOPS. The 448-core number forces document 07's parallelism to be designed for hundreds of threads from the start, including NUMA placement, which is where most engines that were designed for 16 cores fall over.

## 1.6 The GPU question

[**Rethinking Analytical Processing in the GPU Era**](https://vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p12-yogatama.pdf). Yogatama, Yang, Kristensen, Sarda, Kim, Cockcroft, Teng, Patterson, Kimball, McKinney, Gong and Yu, CIDR 2026, [arXiv 2508.04701](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04701). The system is [Sirius](https://github.com/sirius-db/sirius), a GPU-native engine that plugs into DuckDB as an extension through Substrait, reusing DuckDB's parser, optimizer and scans, with silent CPU fallback for unsupported operators and out-of-core tiering across GPU, host and disk.

Measured: 8.3x and 7.4x better cost efficiency than DuckDB on TPC-H and ClickBench, normalizing by running the CPU systems on an `m7i.16xlarge` at the same 3.20 dollars per hour as a GH200 instance, and 7x raw speedup at equal rental cost. On ClickBench Q28, the regex query, cuDF's JIT string transformation framework gave 13x over the precompiled API at 85 percent warp occupancy. NVIDIA reports the ClickBench cost-efficiency record on a 1.50 dollar per hour GH200 with at least 7.2x better cost efficiency than the top CPU systems.

Also in this space at CIDR 2026: **Theseus** (Aramburu and a very long author list), a distributed GPU platform optimized for data movement, and **Data Movement-Aware GPU Sharing for Data-Intensive Systems** (Jiang, Nicholson, Sanca and Ailamaki). And at VLDB 2026, [From Custom-Fit to Portable](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07632), which is the Bespoke OLAP idea applied to GPU execution.

**Forces:** nothing structural, deliberately. The design keeps operator interfaces free of CPU-specific assumptions so that a `rudb-gpu` crate is possible, per document 18, and it does not build the engine around a GPU. Document 02 states the reasoning: the embedded, dependency-free property is why DuckDB is used and it is not negotiable. The Q28 result is however a direct hint about where our own regex path should go, and document 07.6 acts on it.

## 1.7 What the incumbents actually do

**DuckDB.** 2048-value vectors (`STANDARD_VECTOR_SIZE`), 122,880-row row groups which is exactly 2048 times 60, morsel-driven parallelism inherited from HyPer, and a `Vector` type with flat, constant, dictionary and sequence representations that already permits a limited form of compressed execution. Compression set is constant, RLE, bit packing, frame of reference, dictionary, FSST, ALP, Chimp, Patas and Zstd. File header is a checksum, the magic bytes `DUCK`, and a storage version. Rough disk expectation from their own docs: 100 GB of CSV becomes about 25 GB. Document 12 is the full compatibility analysis.

**ClickHouse.** 8192-row granules with a sparse primary index that stores one mark per granule, so the index for a billion-row table is kilobytes and lives entirely in RAM; the `ORDER BY` clause is the index. Skip indices (minmax, set, bloom) as a second pruning layer with a tunable `GRANULARITY`. Projections, which are alternative physically sorted copies of the table stored inside the part directory, chosen by the optimizer on estimated I/O. `max_block_size` of 65,536 for execution blocks, runtime CPU dispatch across scalar, AVX2 and AVX-512 kernels with SSE 4.2 as the floor, LLVM runtime compilation for hot expressions and multi-key sorts, and sort-based aggregation when the grouping keys prefix the primary key.

The layered pruning model is the thing to take. Partition eliminates partitions, the sparse primary index eliminates granules, skip indices eliminate more granules, and only then does anything get read. Document 05.4 adopts the structure and document 09.4 adopts projections under a different name.

**Umbra.** Covered in 1.3 and 1.5. Worth adding that it integrates worst-case optimal joins into the optimizer for subplans with large intermediates, and that its User-Defined Operators are compiled with Clang at -O3 to ELF objects with the bitcode retained so that LLVM can inline across the query and UDO boundary.

**DataFusion.** The Rust reference point. [55.0.0 shipped August 2026](https://datafusion.apache.org/blog/output/2026/08/25/datafusion-55.0.0/) with 877 commits from 175 contributors, range partitioning, `MERGE INTO`, and runtime row-group pruning. 54.0.0 in June 2026 added morsel-driven Parquet scans, replacing fixed partition assignment with dynamic pulling of small morsels, measured at up to 2x on skewed scans such as ClickBench. 53.0.0 in April 2026 focused on planning overhead and LIMIT-aware Parquet pruning.

DataFusion is the honest comparison for "what does a good Rust engine get you today" and the number is instructive: **45.57 seconds hot on ClickBench at `c6a.4xlarge`, against DuckDB's 26.25.** Polars is at 45.35 and Velox at 81.51 on the same board. The Rust and Arrow ecosystem, as of today, is roughly 1.7x behind DuckDB on this workload, not ahead of it. Anyone starting a Rust analytical engine should know that number before they start, and document 02 treats closing it as a prerequisite rather than an achievement.

## 1.9 The summary judgement

Three things are true at once and the project has to hold all three.

The literature says the order of magnitude exists and says where it is: in the physical layout of the data, not in the execution of the operators. Bespoke OLAP proved it by construction and priced the alternative at 26 percent.

The benchmark board says the Rust and Arrow ecosystem is currently 1.7x behind DuckDB on the workload in question, so the starting position is worse than it feels from inside the Rust community, and the first 2x is catch-up rather than progress.

The hardware analysis says the machine we are optimizing for is one large box with hundreds of cores, stagnant per-dollar NVMe, and a memory system that is not getting dramatically faster. That combination means the winning move is to touch fewer bytes, which is the same conclusion the Bespoke OLAP ablation reached from a completely different direction, and the agreement between those two lines of evidence is the strongest single argument that this project is aimed at the right thing.
